# Feeney Triangle — Formal Conjectures, Lemmas, and Theorems

This document applies strict labeling discipline throughout:

- **Observation** — a specific fact noted from data (one instance, or a finite
  set of checked instances). Carries no claim of generality.
- **Conjecture** — a precise, general, falsifiable statement believed true
  on the basis of evidence, but **not proven**.
- **Lemma** — a proven statement used to build toward a theorem.
- **Theorem** — a general statement with a **complete, valid proof** below it.
- **Proof** — the argument itself. If a full proof isn't achieved, the
  result stays a Conjecture, however strong the evidence.

Notation is unchanged from the prior derivation: `s` = start value,
`c` = constant, `n` = row (1-indexed), `k` = position in row (1≤k≤n),
`d = n−k` = distance from the right edge.

## Part I — Theorems (proven)

### Theorem 1 (Column-1 closed forms)

For every operation, column 1 has the closed forms already established:
`s+(n-1)c` (add), `s-(n-1)c` (subtract), `s·c^(n-1)` (multiply),
`s/c^(n-1)` (divide), `s^(c^(n-1))` (power).

**Proof.** Column 1 obeys the univariate recurrence `a(n,1) = a(n-1,1) OP c`,
`a(1,1)=s`, independent of every other entry in the triangle. This is
directly solvable by unrolling `n-1` times for each operation — e.g. for
addition, `a(n,1) = s + c + c + ... + c` (`n-1` copies) `= s+(n-1)c`; for
multiplication, `n-1` repeated multiplications by `c` give `s·c^(n-1)`;
for exponentiation, `n-1` repeated exponentiations by `c` give the tower
`s^(c^(n-1))` (proof by induction: `a(n,1)=a(n-1,1)^c`, and if
`a(n-1,1)=s^(c^(n-2))` then `a(n,1) = (s^(c^(n-2)))^c = s^(c^(n-1))`). ∎

*(This restates and formalizes what was empirically verified —15 exact
matches for the power case, 216+ for add/subtract — in the prior session;
the derivation above is the actual proof, the checks are confirmation.)*

### Theorem 2 (Addition — full closed form)

`a(n,k) = 2^(k-2)·[2s + (2n-k-1)c]` for all `1≤k≤n`.

**Proof.** Since the recurrence is linear in `(s,c)`, write
`a(n,k)=A(n,k)s+B(n,k)c`. Setting `c=0` collapses column 1 to the constant
`s`, and induction on `A(n,k)=A(n,k-1)+A(n-1,k-1)` with `A(n,1)=1` gives
`A(n,k)=2^(k-1)` (base case `2^0=1`; step: if `A(n,k-1)=A(n-1,k-1)=2^(k-2)`
then `A(n,k)=2·2^(k-2)=2^(k-1)`). Setting `s=0` gives `B(n,1)=n-1`; assume
inductively `B(n,k-1)` is affine in `n`, `B(n,k-1)=p_{k-1}n+q_{k-1}`. Then
`B(n,k)=B(n,k-1)+B(n-1,k-1)=p_{k-1}n+q_{k-1}+p_{k-1}(n-1)+q_{k-1}
=2p_{k-1}n+(2q_{k-1}-p_{k-1})`, which is again affine, confirming the
induction and giving `p_k=2p_{k-1}`, `q_k=2q_{k-1}-p_{k-1}` with
`p_1=1,q_1=-1`. Solving: `p_k=2^{k-1}`. For `q_k`, substitute the ansatz
`q_k = A·2^k - k·2^{k-2}` into the recurrence and solve for `A` using
`q_1=-1`, giving `A=-1/4`, hence `q_k = -(k+1)2^{k-2}`. So
`B(n,k)=2^{k-1}n-(k+1)2^{k-2}=2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)`. Combining,
`a(n,k)=2^{k-1}s+2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)c=2^{k-2}[2s+(2n-k-1)c]`. ∎

*(Verified exactly against the live engine: 216 checks, 6 parameter pairs
× all entries through row 8, zero mismatches.)*

### Theorem 3 (Subtraction — full closed form)

`a(n,1)=s-(n-1)c`, `a(n,2)=-c`, `a(n,k)=0` for `k≥3`.

**Proof.** Column 1 as in Theorem 1. For `k≥2`, if column `k-1` is affine
in `n` with slope `p`, i.e. `a(n,k-1)=pn+q`, then
`a(n,k)=a(n,k-1)-a(n-1,k-1)=[pn+q]-[p(n-1)+q]=p` — a **constant**, not
affine (degree drops by one, unlike addition where it stays affine and
the slope doubles). Column 1 has slope `p_1=-c`, so column 2 is the
constant `-c`. Applying the same step once more: since column 2 is
constant (slope 0), column 3 is the constant `0`, and every column
thereafter remains `0` (a constant minus itself). ∎

*(Verified: 120 checks, exact. Corrects an earlier false shortcut in this
conversation, where subtraction was wrongly assumed to be "addition with
a negated constant" — that only holds for column 1, as this proof makes
explicit by showing the *mechanism* differs beyond it.)*

### Theorem 4 (Log-isomorphism: Multiplication ≅ Addition)

For `s,c>0`, `log a_mult(n,k) = a_add(n,k)` where `a_add` is generated by
Addition with `start'=log s`, `constant'=log c`, for every `(n,k)` such
that `a_mult(n,k)` is finite.

**Proof.** By induction on the construction. Base case: `log a_mult(1,1) =
log s = a_add(1,1)`. Inductive step for column 1: if
`log a_mult(n-1,1)=a_add(n-1,1)`, then since `a_mult(n,1)=a_mult(n-1,1)·c`
and `log` turns products into sums,
`log a_mult(n,1) = log a_mult(n-1,1)+log c = a_add(n-1,1)+c' = a_add(n,1)`.
For `k≥2`: `a_mult(n,k)=a_mult(n,k-1)·a_mult(n-1,k-1)`, and (given `s,c>0`,
every entry in a multiplication triangle stays positive by induction, so
`log` is defined throughout) `log a_mult(n,k) = log a_mult(n,k-1) +
log a_mult(n-1,k-1) = a_add(n,k-1)+a_add(n-1,k-1) = a_add(n,k)`, matching
the addition recurrence exactly. ∎

*(Verified within finite floating-point range: matches the log-transformed
multiplication triangle to the addition triangle to ~1e-9 relative
tolerance across 6 test cases; divergence only appears once `a_mult`
itself overflows to `Infinity`, which is a floating-point representation
limit — see Theorem 12 — not a failure of this proof.)*

### Theorem 5 (Log-isomorphism: Division ≅ Subtraction)

For `s,c>0`, `log a_div(n,k) = a_sub(n,k)` (Subtraction, `start'=log s`,
`constant'=log c`).

**Proof.** Identical structure to Theorem 4, using `log(a/b)=log a - log b`
in place of `log(ab)=log a+log b`. ∎

### Theorem 6 (Multiplication — full closed form)

`a(n,k) = s^(2^(k-1)) · c^(2^(k-2)(2n-k-1))` for `s,c>0`.

**Proof.** Substitute Theorem 2's formula into Theorem 4's isomorphism and
exponentiate both sides: `a_mult(n,k) = exp(a_add(n,k)) =
exp(2^{k-2}[2 log s + (2n-k-1) log c]) = s^{2^{k-1}}·c^{2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)}`. ∎

*(Verified: 68 checks against the live engine, exact — including
`a(4,4)=1,048,576` and `a(6,6)≈5.19×10^33` for the canonical (2,2) case.)*

### Theorem 7 (Division — full closed form)

`a(n,1)=s/c^(n-1)`, `a(n,2)=1/c`, `a(n,k)=1` for `k≥3` (`s,c>0`).

**Proof.** Substitute Theorem 3 into Theorem 5 and exponentiate:
`c'^{-(n-1)}·s → s/c^{n-1}`, `c'^{-1}→1/c`, `c'^0→1`, where `c'=log c`. ∎

*(Verified: 128 checks, exact.)*

### Theorem 8 (Perfect squares under multiplication, k≥3)

For positive integers `s,c`, every entry in column `k≥3` of the
Multiplication triangle is a perfect square.

**Proof.** By Theorem 6, `a(n,k) = s^{2^{k-1}} · c^{2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)}`. The
exponent of `s` is `2^{k-1}`, even for every `k≥2`, so `s^{2^{k-1}} =
(s^{2^{k-2}})^2` is always a perfect square. For the exponent of `c`,
`2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)`: at `k=3` this is `4n-8=4(n-2)`, an integer multiple of
4, hence even, so `c^{4(n-2)}=(c^{2(n-2)})^2` is a perfect square. For
general `k≥3`, `2^{k-2}≥2` is itself even, so `2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)` is even
regardless of the parity of `(2n-k-1)`, making `c^{2^{k-2}(2n-k-1)}` a
perfect square for every `k≥3`. The product of two perfect squares is a
perfect square, giving the result. ∎

*(Verified with exact BigInt arithmetic — not floating point — on values
up to 78 digits: 100% match, no false positives, in the prior session.)*

### Theorem 9 (Diagonal formulas)

Fixing `d=n-k`: Addition gives `a(n,n-d)=2^{n-d-2}[2s+(n+d-1)c]`;
Multiplication gives the analogous exponentiated form; Subtraction and
Division diagonals lock to their column-3 constant (`0` or `1`) once
`n-d≥3`.

**Proof.** Direct substitution `k=n-d` into Theorems 2, 3, 6, 7. ∎

*(Verified: 108 checks, exact, including the subtraction diagonal
sequences `d=0→[10,-1,0,0,...]` etc.)*

### Theorem 10 (Matrix/operator formulation)

Addition: `a(n,k) = a(n,1) + Σ_{j=1}^{k-1} a(n-1,j)` (prefix sum).
Multiplication: `a(n,k) = a(n,1) · Π_{j=1}^{k-1} a(n-1,j)` (prefix product).

**Proof (addition case).** By induction on `k`. Base case `k=1` is trivial
(empty sum). Assume `a(n,k-1)=a(n,1)+Σ_{j=1}^{k-2}a(n-1,j)`. Then by
definition `a(n,k)=a(n,k-1)+a(n-1,k-1) = a(n,1)+Σ_{j=1}^{k-2}a(n-1,j) +
a(n-1,k-1) = a(n,1)+Σ_{j=1}^{k-1}a(n-1,j)`. ∎ The multiplication case
follows by the identical argument with `+`→`×`, or via Theorem 4.

*(Verified: 43 checks, exact.)*

### Theorem 11 (Row-direction non-polynomiality)

Fix `n` and an operation of Addition with `c≠0`. There is no polynomial
`P` of any fixed degree such that `a(n,k)=P(k)` holds for all `k` in
`1..n`, uniformly as `n→∞` (i.e., no single fixed-degree polynomial
formula in `k` works across arbitrarily long rows).

**Proof.** From Theorem 2, `a(n,k) = (\alpha k + \beta)\cdot 2^k` for
constants `α,β` depending on `n` and `c` (with `α≠0` whenever `c≠0`).
Compute the ratio of consecutive terms:
`a(n,k+1)/a(n,k) = [(α(k+1)+β)/(αk+β)]·2 → 2` as `k→∞`. But for *any*
polynomial `Q` of degree `D≥1`, `Q(k+1)/Q(k) → 1` as `k→∞` (the leading
term dominates: `Q(k)∼c_D k^D` gives ratio `∼((k+1)/k)^D→1`); for `D=0`
(constant `Q`) the ratio is identically `1`. Neither limit is `2`, so
`a(n,·)` cannot equal any fixed-degree polynomial for large `k`. ∎

*(This upgrades the numeric polynomial-regression check from the prior
session — which only showed *degree 5 doesn't reach R²=1 on 10 points*,
weak evidence on its own since any 10 points admit a degree-9 fit — to an
actual proof of impossibility.)*

### Theorem 12 (IEEE-754 absorption threshold)

Let `x,y` be nonzero finite binary64 (double-precision) values of the
same sign, `|x|≥|y|`. If `|x|/|y| ≥ 2^53` (`= 9{,}007{,}199{,}254{,}740{,}992
≈9.007×10^15`), the correctly-rounded floating-point sum satisfies
`fl(x+y)=x` exactly — `y` is entirely absorbed and lost.

**Proof.** Binary64 stores a 52-bit mantissa plus an implicit leading bit,
giving 53 bits of significance. To add `x+y`, `y` must first be aligned to
`x`'s exponent. If `|y| < |x|·2^{-53}`, every significant bit of `y`,
once aligned, falls strictly beyond `x`'s 53rd (least significant)
representable bit. Under round-to-nearest (the IEEE-754 default), the
correctly rounded result is therefore `x` itself; `y` contributes nothing
representable. (Standard result; see Goldberg, "What Every Computer
Scientist Should Know About Floating-Point Arithmetic," 1991.) ∎

**This is the exact mechanism behind the automated-testing session's
finding.** `2^53≈9.007×10^15` sits precisely inside the empirically
observed break window (exact between `1e15`, still correct, and `1e16`,
broken) — confirmed directly: `1e15+1` and even `9.007×10^15+1` compute
exactly, while `1e16+1` rounds to `1e16`. This is not a coincidence to be
further investigated; it is the textbook mechanism, now confirmed to
match this specific implementation to the bit.

### Theorem 13 (Feeney Triangle ≠ Bell Triangle, for any operation or parameters)

No choice of operation, `s`, or `c` makes the Feeney Triangle identical
to the Bell/Peirce/Aitken triangle for all `n`.

**Proof.** It suffices to show column 1 (the Bell numbers, in Bell's
triangle) cannot equal Feeney's column 1 under any operation. Feeney's
column 1 is, by Theorem 1: affine in `n` (add/subtract), geometric in `n`
(multiply/divide), or a power tower with *linear* log-log growth
(`log log a(n,1) = (n-1)\log c + \log\log s`, exponentiation). The Bell
numbers `B_n` satisfy the classical fact `B_{n+1}/B_n \to \infty` as
`n\to\infty` (superexponential growth — standard, e.g. from the
recurrence `B_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}B_k`, which is bounded below
by the single term `\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}B_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}`,
and central binomial coefficients alone grow like `2^n/\sqrt n\to\infty`).
- Affine and geometric sequences both have *bounded* `a(n+1)/a(n)` (→1 or
  a fixed constant respectively) — neither matches `\to\infty`.
- The power tower has `\log a(n,1)` growing *geometrically* with fixed
  ratio `c`, so `\log\log a(n,1)` is *exactly linear* in `n` (constant
  increments `\log c`). But `B_{n+1}/B_n\to\infty` forces
  `\log B_{n+1}-\log B_n\to\infty` (unbounded, growing increments), so
  `\log B_n`'s increments are unbounded — inconsistent with the *constant*
  increments of `\log\log`(power tower). Hence the two cannot agree for
  all large `n`.

Since none of Feeney's five column-1 forms can equal the Bell numbers for
all large `n`, and Bell's triangle's defining feature *is* its column 1,
no parameter choice makes the two triangles identical. ∎

*(This formalizes and settles, for the closest structural relative found
in the literature search, the open question from that session. The other
comparisons — Pascal's, Stern's, Catalan's — were left as evidenced
Observations, not theorems, below; the arguments are quicker but were not
carried to the same completeness.)*

## Part II — Conjectures (not proven)

### Conjecture A — No elementary closed form for Exponentiation, k≥2

**Statement.** For the Exponentiation operation, there is no formula for
`a(n,k)` (`k≥2`) built from finite compositions of `+, -, ×, ÷, ^, log,
exp` in `n`, `k`, `s`, `c`.

**Supporting evidence.** Column 2 of the canonical `(s,c)=(2,2)` case is
`[16, 65536, 3.4×10^38, ∞]`; consecutive ratios are `[4096, 5.2×10^33]`
(wildly non-constant, ruling out geometric); `log-log` of the sequence is
`[1.02, 2.41, 4.49, ∞]` (still not linear or otherwise patterned). One
log doesn't reduce it to a known operation's recurrence (Theorem 4/5's
argument fails here because the recurrence
`log a(n,k) = a(n-1,k-1)\cdot\log a(n,k-1)` mixes a raw value with a
logged one — structurally unlike any of the other four operations).

**Confidence: Low-moderate (30%).** The empirical signal is real but this
is fundamentally a non-existence claim over an open-ended space of
"elementary" formulas, which empirical failure to find one doesn't rule
out.

**Assumptions.** "Closed form" is restricted to elementary functions;
richer objects (special functions, infinite series, recursive/implicit
definitions) are excluded by construction, or this conjecture is
trivially false (the recursive definition itself always "counts").

**Proof strategy (why this is hard).** A genuine non-existence proof
would likely require differential-algebra / Liouville-theory-style
methods (as used to prove, e.g., that `∫e^{x^2}dx` has no elementary
closed form) adapted to discrete recurrences — a nontrivial research
undertaking, not attempted here.

### Conjecture B — No simple bivariate generating function for Add/Multiply

**Statement.** The bivariate generating function `F(x,y)=Σ_n R_n(y)x^n`
(where `R_n(y)=Σ_k a(n,k)y^k`) for the Addition (or, isomorphically,
Multiplication) triangle has no closed form as a simple rational function
of `x,y` — unlike Pascal's triangle, whose equivalent GF is
`1/(1-x-xy)`.

**Supporting evidence.** The row-polynomial recursion derived from the
defining recurrence,
`R_n(y) = [a(n,1)y - a(n,n)y^{n+1} + yR_{n-1}(y)]/(1-y)`,
carries a boundary term `a(n,n)y^{n+1}` whose exponent grows with `n` —
this is exactly the mechanism that keeps Pascal's triangle's analogous
derivation *from* having such a term (Pascal's boundary values are the
fixed constant 1, not an `n`-dependent quantity), and is why that
derivation collapses to a simple rational function while this one
doesn't appear to.

**Confidence: Moderate (50%).** The obstruction is real and precisely
located, but "no simple GF exists" wasn't exhaustively checked against
every possible substitution or transform (e.g. exponential generating
functions, or GFs in a transformed variable) that might still work.

**Proof strategy.** Substitute Theorem 9's exact diagonal formula for
`a(n,n)` into the recursion above and attempt to sum the resulting
explicit two-part series in closed form; if a genuinely non-rational but
still "nice" (e.g. algebraic) closed form emerges, the conjecture would
be refined rather than confirmed. Not attempted here.

### Conjecture C — The well-conditioned regime is universal

**Statement.** For all five operations, any parameter combination with
`|s|` and `|c|` both in `[10^{-6}, 10^6]`, and any row count up to at
least the tested maximum (300), every one of this conversation's nine
tested algebraic conjectures (arithmetic/geometric columns, subtraction/
division collapse, etc.) holds in the actual floating-point
implementation, not just in exact arithmetic.

**Supporting evidence.** 296,620 automated trials in the prior session,
zero counterexamples inside this exact range; all 15,671 counterexamples
found fell outside it. Theorem 12 gives the precise mechanism for the
single-step case (column-1 absorption).

**Confidence: High (85%).** Strong empirical support plus a proven
mechanism for the simplest case.

**Assumptions.** "Holds" means matches the exact-arithmetic closed form
to the 1e-6 relative tolerance used throughout this conversation's
testing — a looser bar than bit-exactness.

**Proof strategy.** Extend Theorem 12 from a single absorption event to a
full forward-error bound across a chain of up to 300 operations, via
standard summation error analysis (the accumulated relative error after
`m` floating-point operations is bounded by roughly `m\cdot\varepsilon_{mach}`
for well-scaled inputs, `\varepsilon_{mach}\approx2.22\times10^{-16}`);
for `m\le300`, this bound (`\approx6.7\times10^{-14}`) sits comfortably
under the `10^{-6}` tolerance whenever no single-step absorption or
overflow event occurs — which Theorem 12 shows requires the `1e-6..1e6`
range to avoid. This sketch is plausible but wasn't carried out as a
complete formal induction here.

## Part III — Observations (not general claims)

- **Observation 1.** The rightmost diagonal of the Addition triangle at
  `(s,c)=(1,1)` — `1,3,8,20,48,112,256,576,...` — is exactly OEIS A001792,
  confirmed by algebraic match of formulas (`(n+2)2^{n-1}` vs. Theorem 9's
  diagonal formula at `d=0`), not just numeric coincidence.
- **Observation 2.** No search (OEIS, MathWorld, Wikipedia, arXiv, general
  web) turned up a named construction identical to the Feeney Triangle as
  a whole. This is evidence, not proof of non-existence — it is not
  elevated to a Conjecture here because "no construction exists anywhere
  in mathematics" has no meaningful proof strategy from search results
  alone.
- **Observation 3.** Bell's triangle's `k≥2` recurrence rule (`sum of
  left neighbor and above-left neighbor`) is *identical in form* to
  Feeney's Addition `k≥2` rule — the only structural difference is the
  row-starting rule (Bell copies the previous row's last value; Feeney
  combines the previous row's first value with an external constant).
  Theorem 13 shows this difference is enough to make the triangles
  provably distinct, but the shared sub-structure is worth noting as
  the closest relative found.
